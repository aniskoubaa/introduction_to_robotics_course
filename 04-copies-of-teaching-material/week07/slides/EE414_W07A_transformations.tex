% ===============================================================
\section{Why frames exist}
% ===============================================================

\begin{frame}{A number with no frame is not information}
  \begin{beamercolorbox}[sep=8pt,center,rounded=true]{palette secondary}
    \small The LiDAR reports an obstacle at \textbf{2.1\,m, bearing $30^\circ$}.
  \end{beamercolorbox}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \small
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
    \toprule
    2.1\,m from what? & The laser, which sits 10\,cm forward and 20\,cm up from the robot's centre \\
    $30^\circ$ from what? & The laser's zero direction, which may not be the robot's forward \\
    So where is it? & \textbf{Unanswerable} until you say what the numbers are measured from \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  A \textbf{frame} is that ``measured from''. Every position, velocity and orientation in
  robotics belongs to exactly one, and a value quoted without its frame is not wrong --- it is
  meaningless.
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{The frames on a small mobile robot}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tikzpicture}[node distance=0.32cm, font=\scriptsize]
    \node[world] (map) {\texttt{map}};

    \node[act,  below=of odom] (base) {\texttt{base\_link}};
    \node[sense, below left=0.35cm and 0.6cm of base]  (scan) {\texttt{base\_scan}};
    \node[sense, below right=0.35cm and 0.6cm of base] (imu)  {\texttt{imu\_link}};

    \draw[flowarrow] (map)  -- (odom);
    \draw[flowarrow] (odom) -- (base);
    \draw[flowarrow] (base) -- (scan);
    \draw[flowarrow] (base) -- (imu);
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{2.1cm}p{8.6cm}@{}}
    \toprule
    \texttt{map} & The world. Fixed, never drifts. Jumps when localization corrects itself. \\
    \texttt{odom} & Where the robot thinks it has driven. Smooth, but drifts forever. \\
    \texttt{base\_link} & The robot's own centre. Everything on the robot hangs off this. \\
    \texttt{base\_scan} & The laser. Bolted on, so this link never changes. \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{The two questions this lecture answers}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Expression.} I have a point in \texttt{base\_scan}. What is it in
          \texttt{base\_link}?
    \item \textbf{Composition.} I know \texttt{base\_scan} relative to \texttt{base\_link}, and
          \texttt{base\_link} relative to \texttt{odom}. What is \texttt{base\_scan} relative
          to \texttt{odom}?
  \end{enumerate}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \small
  Both have one answer, and it is a matrix. Once you can write it down and multiply it in the
  right order, obstacle avoidance, mapping, localization and navigation all become bookkeeping.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{inthefield}
    \footnotesize
    Almost every ``the robot turned the wrong way'' bug in this course will turn out to be a
    frame error --- a transform applied backwards, or applied in the wrong order. Not a control
    bug. A frame bug.
  \end{inthefield}
\end{frame}

% ===============================================================
\section{Rotation}
% ===============================================================

\begin{frame}{Rotating a point in the plane}
  \small
  Rotate $\mathbf{p}=(x,y)$ by angle $\theta$ about the origin:
  \[
    \mathbf{p}' \;=\; R(\theta)\,\mathbf{p},
    \qquad
    R(\theta)=
    \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\[2pt] \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}
  \]

  \vspace{0.4em}
  Read the columns: they are where the old axes end up.
  \[
    R(\theta)\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}
      = \begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta\\ \sin\theta\end{bmatrix},
    \qquad
    R(\theta)\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}
      = \begin{bmatrix}-\sin\theta\\ \cos\theta\end{bmatrix}
  \]

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{underhood}[A check you can do in your head]
    \footnotesize
    Put $\theta = 90^\circ$. Then $\hat{x}=(1,0)$ maps to $(0,1)$ --- forward becomes left, which
    is what turning left should do. If a rotation matrix ever surprises you, test it with
    $90^\circ$ and one axis.
  \end{underhood}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Three properties that matter}
  \small
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{3.4cm}p{7.3cm}@{}}
    \toprule
    $R^{\mathsf T}R = I$ & The columns are unit length and perpendicular. Rotation does not
      stretch or skew. \\[0.4em]
    $R^{-1} = R^{\mathsf T}$ & \textbf{The inverse is free.} Undoing a rotation is a transpose,
      not a matrix inversion. \\[0.4em]
    $\det R = +1$ & It is a rotation, not a reflection. A $-1$ here means you have accidentally
      mirrored your robot. \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \begin{inthefield}
    \footnotesize
    $R^{-1}=R^{\mathsf T}$ is not a curiosity --- it is why a robot can invert transforms
    thousands of times a second on a small processor. Every lookup in the opposite direction
    uses it.
  \end{inthefield}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{The confusion everyone has once}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \column{0.47\textwidth}
    \begin{block}{\small Rotating the \emph{point}}
      \footnotesize
      The frame stays. The object moves.\\[0.3em]
      Turn the obstacle $30^\circ$ about the robot.\\[0.3em]
      $\mathbf{p}' = R(\theta)\,\mathbf{p}$
    \end{block}

    \column{0.47\textwidth}
    \begin{block}{\small Re-expressing in a rotated \emph{frame}}
      \footnotesize
      The object stays. The observer turns.\\[0.3em]
      The obstacle did not move; the laser is mounted $30^\circ$ off.\\[0.3em]
      $\mathbf{p}' = R(\theta)^{\mathsf T}\,\mathbf{p}$
    \end{block}
  \end{columns}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \centering
  \textbf{Same matrix. Opposite direction.} These differ by a transpose, and mixing them up
  produces a robot that turns away from the goal instead of towards it.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \footnotesize
  In this course we almost always want the second one: nothing moved, we are changing where we
  are looking from.
\end{frame}

% ===============================================================
\section{Rotation and translation together}
% ===============================================================

\begin{frame}{The obvious formula, and why we abandon it}
  \small
  A rigid transform is a rotation then a translation:
  \[ \mathbf{p}_A \;=\; R\,\mathbf{p}_B + \mathbf{t} \]

  \vspace{0.4em}
  Correct. Now chain three of them:
  \[ \mathbf{p}_A \;=\; R_1(R_2(R_3\mathbf{p}_D + \mathbf{t}_3) + \mathbf{t}_2) + \mathbf{t}_1 \]

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
    \toprule
    Composing two of them & Not a multiplication. A rule you must remember. \\
    Inverting one & $\mathbf{p}_B = R^{\mathsf T}(\mathbf{p}_A - \mathbf{t})$ --- easy to get wrong \\
    Chaining four & Unreadable, and unmaintainable \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \centering We want one object that composes by multiplication. That is the next slide.
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Homogeneous coordinates}
  \small
  Add a 1 to the point, and a row to the matrix:
  \[
    \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{p}_A \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
    =
    \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} R & \mathbf{t} \\ \mathbf{0}^{\mathsf T} & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{T}
    \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{p}_B \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
  \]

  \vspace{0.3em}
  In the plane that is $3\times3$; in 3D, $4\times4$. Rotation and translation now live in one
  object.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
    \toprule
    Compose & $T_{AC} = T_{AB}\,T_{BC}$ \qquad (matrix multiplication) \\
    Invert  & $T^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} R^{\mathsf T} & -R^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{t} \\
                        \mathbf{0}^{\mathsf T} & 1\end{bmatrix}$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \footnotesize
  The extra 1 is what lets a translation --- which is not linear --- be written as a matrix.
  That is the entire trick.
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Notation discipline, or you will lose an afternoon}
  \begin{beamercolorbox}[sep=8pt,center,rounded=true]{palette primary}
    $T_{AB}$ \;=\; the pose of frame $B$ expressed in frame $A$\\
    \;=\; the thing that turns $B$-coordinates into $A$-coordinates
  \end{beamercolorbox}

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \small
  Written this way, composition reads itself --- \textbf{the inner indices cancel}:
  \[ T_{AC} \;=\; T_{AB}\;T_{BC}
     \qquad\qquad
     T_{BA} \;=\; T_{AB}^{-1} \]

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \begin{underhood}[Use this as a type check]
    \footnotesize
    If the inner subscripts do not match, the product is meaningless --- no matter how well the
    dimensions line up. $T_{AB}T_{CD}$ compiles, runs, and is nonsense. This notation is the
    only defence, and it costs nothing to adopt.
  \end{underhood}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Worked: where is that obstacle, really?}
  \small
  The laser sits 10\,cm forward and 20\,cm up from the robot centre, mounted straight.
  It reports a point at $(2.1, 0)$ in \texttt{base\_scan}.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \[
    T_{\text{base\_link},\,\text{base\_scan}}
    = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0.10 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
    \qquad
    \mathbf{p}_{\text{scan}} = \begin{bmatrix} 2.1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
  \]

  \vspace{0.3em}
  \[
    \mathbf{p}_{\text{base}} = T_{\text{base\_link},\,\text{base\_scan}}\;\mathbf{p}_{\text{scan}}
      = \begin{bmatrix} 2.20 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
  \]

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \footnotesize
  Ten centimetres. It sounds negligible --- until Week 11, where the robot decides whether it
  fits through a gap. \textbf{Ten centimetres is the difference between passing and scraping.}
\end{frame}

% ===============================================================
\section{Trees, and time}
% ===============================================================

\begin{frame}{Why a tree, and not just a pile of transforms}
  \small
  Every frame has \textbf{exactly one parent}. That single rule buys three things:

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{3.2cm}p{7.5cm}@{}}
    \toprule
    One path & Between any two frames there is exactly one chain, so the answer is unambiguous \\[0.3em]
    Automatic lookup & The library walks the chain and multiplies; you never write the product \\[0.3em]
    Errors are findable & Two publishers of the same parent--child link is detectable, and is
      always a bug \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \begin{inthefield}
    \footnotesize
    ``Two nodes publishing \texttt{odom} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{base\_link}'' is one of the most
    common faults on a real robot: the transform flickers between two answers and the robot
    twitches. Because it is a tree, the tooling can tell you --- which it could not if any frame
    could have several parents.
  \end{inthefield}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Three dimensions: the same idea, one hard part}
  \small
  In 3D the transform is $4\times4$ and everything above still holds. The only genuinely new
  difficulty is \textbf{how to write the rotation}:

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{2.4cm}p{2.0cm}p{6.2cm}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Form} & \textbf{Numbers} & \textbf{The catch} \\
    \midrule
    Matrix & 9 & Correct, composes cleanly, but 9 numbers for 3 degrees of freedom \\[0.3em]
    Euler angles & 3 & Readable --- and suffers \emph{gimbal lock}: at certain orientations two
      axes collapse and you lose a degree of freedom \\[0.3em]
    Quaternion & 4 & No gimbal lock, cheap to compose and interpolate; not human-readable \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \textbf{ROS 2 stores orientations as quaternions}, everywhere, for exactly this reason.

  \vspace{0.3em}
  \footnotesize
  You will convert yaw $\leftrightarrow$ quaternion constantly and read the quaternion itself
  almost never. That is normal, and it is not a gap in your understanding.
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{A transform is only true at an instant}
  \small
  \texttt{base\_link} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{base\_scan} is bolted metal: constant forever.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \texttt{odom} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{base\_link} changes every time the wheels turn. Asking
  ``where was the robot?'' without saying \emph{when} has no answer.

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tikzpicture}[font=\scriptsize]
    \draw[->,thick,draw=primary] (0,0) -- (9,0) node[right] {time};
    \foreach \x in {1,2.6,4.2,5.8,7.4} \fill[secondary] (\x,0) circle (2pt);
    \node[align=center, font=\tiny] at (4.2,0.45) {transforms arrive here};
    \draw[danger, very thick] (6.6,-0.25) -- (6.6,0.25);
    \node[text=danger, font=\tiny, align=center] at (6.6,-0.6) {your scan\\ was taken here};
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{underhood}[Why Week 2's \texttt{Header} exists]
    \footnotesize
    The library stores a short history and answers ``what was this transform at time $t$?'', by
    interpolating between the samples on either side. That is only possible because every
    message carries the time its data was \emph{measured}. This is Week 1's ``nothing is
    instantaneous'' made concrete.
  \end{underhood}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Two failures that come from ignoring time}
  \small
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{4.3cm}p{6.5cm}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{What you asked for} & \textbf{What happens} \\
    \midrule
    A transform at a time \emph{later} than anything received
      & ``Lookup would require extrapolation into the future.'' The library refuses to guess ---
        correctly. \\[0.4em]
    A transform at a time already discarded from the buffer
      & ``Lookup would require extrapolation into the past.'' Your node was too slow, or the
        buffer is too short. \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \centering
  These two messages will be among the most familiar strings of your semester. \textbf{They are
  not bugs in the library.} They are the library declining to invent data.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \footnotesize You will cause both on purpose in session B.
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{What to carry out of this lecture}
  \begin{enumerate}\small
    \item Every measurement belongs to a frame. A number without one is meaningless.
    \item A rigid transform is one matrix: rotation and translation together.
    \item $T_{AC} = T_{AB}T_{BC}$ --- \textbf{inner indices cancel}. Use it as a type check.
    \item $T_{BA} = T_{AB}^{-1}$, and for the rotation part the inverse is just a transpose.
    \item Frames form a tree: one parent each, one path between any two.
    \item A transform is only valid at an instant, which is why every message is stamped.
  \end{enumerate}
