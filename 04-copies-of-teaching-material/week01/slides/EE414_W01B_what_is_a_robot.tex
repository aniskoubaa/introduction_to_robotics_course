\begin{frame}{A working definition}
  \begin{beamercolorbox}[sep=8pt,center,rounded=true]{palette primary}
    \large A robot \textbf{senses} its environment, \textbf{decides} what to do,\\
    and \textbf{acts} on the physical world.
  \end{beamercolorbox}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  All three are required. Drop any one and you have something else:

  \begin{center}\small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Missing} & \textbf{What you have} & \textbf{Example} \\
    \midrule
    Sensing  & An automaton --- it repeats, it does not react & Washing machine, CNC \\
    Deciding & A teleoperated tool --- a human is the loop    & Surgical master--slave arm \\
    Acting   & A perception or reasoning system               & A vision model, an LLM \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \small The boundary is genuinely fuzzy, and arguing about it is not useful. What is useful
  is knowing \emph{which of the three parts} a given machine is missing.
\end{frame}

    \footnotesize

    switch it off.
  \end{underhood}

\begin{frame}{Why mobile robots, and why now}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Warehousing and logistics} --- the largest deployed fleet of autonomous

    concentrated, and Vision 2030 names industrial automation explicitly. The stack in this

    was rebuilt.
  \end{inthefield}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The sensors, and what each one lies about}
  \small
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{2.2cm}p{3.7cm}p{5.2cm}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Sensor} & \textbf{What it gives you} & \textbf{How it lets you down} \\
    \midrule
    Wheel encoders & How far each wheel turned & Wheels slip. The error \emph{accumulates} and never recovers. \\[0.3em]
    IMU & Angular rate, acceleration & Drifts. Integrate it twice and the answer is fiction. \\[0.3em]

  \end{center}

    \footnotesize
    No single sensor is trustworthy, but their failures are \emph{different}. Combining them
    so the errors cancel is sensor fusion --- Week 9, and why the Kalman filter is here.
  \end{underhood}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The actuator you will spend the semester commanding}
  \small

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    \begin{itemize}\footnotesize
      \item Both wheels same speed $\rightarrow$ straight line
      \item Opposite speeds $\rightarrow$ turn in place
      \item Anything else $\rightarrow$ an arc
    \end{itemize}
    \vspace{0.4em}

    \footnotesize

    once, drive anything.
  \end{inthefield}

\section{Three hard truths}
% ===============================================================

% PAIN BEFORE NOTATION. "x = 4.2 +/- 0.3" is a notation; nobody believes a
% notation. Everybody has already done this experiment with their own legs.
\begin{frame}{Do this now, with your eyes shut}
  \small
  Stand up. Close your eyes. Walk ten steps forward, turn around, walk ten steps back.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \keyline{You are not where you started.}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  You knew how many steps you took. You knew you turned around. You were still wrong by half
  a metre --- because you were \textbf{counting your own motion} instead of looking at the room.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \begin{underhood}[This is exactly what a wheel encoder does]
    \footnotesize
    It counts rotations. If a wheel slips 2\,\% of the time --- carpet, dust, a cable --- then
    after 50\,m of driving the robot is \textbf{a metre} from where it believes it is. And
    nothing in that calculation ever tells it so. The error only grows.
  \end{underhood}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Truth 1 --- every measurement is wrong}
  \small
  A robot never knows where it is. It has a \textbf{belief}, and a measure of how much to
  trust it.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \begin{block}{\small What a beginner writes}
      \footnotesize
      \texttt{x = 4.2} \\[0.3em]
      ``The robot is at 4.2 metres.''
    \end{block}
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \begin{block}{\small What robotics writes}
      \footnotesize
      \texttt{x = 4.2 $\pm$ 0.3} \\[0.3em]
      ``Most likely 4.2, and I would not bet the robot on the third digit.''
    \end{block}
  \end{columns}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \begin{underhood}
    \footnotesize
    This is why Weeks 9--10 are probabilistic. A planner that trusts a wrong position drives
    confidently into a wall --- the confidence is the bug, not the position.
  \end{underhood}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Truth 2 --- nothing is instantaneous}
  \small
  Between the world changing and the wheels responding, there is a chain of delays:

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tikzpicture}[node distance=0.35cm]
    \node[sense, minimum width=1.9cm, font=\tiny, align=center] (a) {LiDAR scan\\ 100\,ms};
    \node[sense, minimum width=1.9cm, right=of a, font=\tiny, align=center] (b) {transmit\\ few ms};
    \node[plan,  minimum width=1.9cm, right=of b, font=\tiny, align=center] (c) {process\\ 20\,ms};
    \node[plan,  minimum width=1.9cm, right=of c, font=\tiny, align=center] (d) {plan\\ 50\,ms};
    \node[act,   minimum width=1.9cm, right=of d, font=\tiny, align=center] (e) {motor\\ responds};
    \draw[dataflow] (a)--(b); \draw[dataflow] (b)--(c);
    \draw[dataflow] (c)--(d); \draw[dataflow] (d)--(e);
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \small
  \textbf{Add them up: about 175\,ms.} At the 0.22\,m/s this course drives at, the robot
  travels \textbf{4\,cm after it has already decided to stop} --- and the thing it saw has
  moved too.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \centering\small You are always steering by a slightly old picture of the world.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{inthefield}
    \footnotesize
    Four centimetres sounds survivable. Multiply the speed by ten, as a warehouse AMR does, and
    it is 40\,cm. This is why a controller that is perfect in a simulator with no delay
    oscillates on real hardware, and why we tune in simulation \emph{with} realistic rates.
  \end{inthefield}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Truth 3 --- everything runs at once}
  \small
  These are not steps in a program. They are all running, always, at different rates:

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}llr@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Job} & \textbf{Must run at} & \textbf{Because} \\
    \midrule
    Read the LiDAR       & 10\,Hz  & that is how fast it spins \\
    Read wheel encoders  & 50\,Hz  & odometry error grows if you miss samples \\
    Run the controller   & 20\,Hz  & smooth motion \\
    Re-plan the path     & 1\,Hz   & planning is expensive \\
    Update the map       & 5\,Hz   & the world changes \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \centering
  \begin{beamercolorbox}[sep=6pt,center,rounded=true]{palette secondary}
    \small So: how do you write \emph{one program} that does all five, at five different rates?
  \end{beamercolorbox}

  \vspace{0.3em}
  \centering \small You do not. That is the whole point of the next section.
\end{frame}

\section{Why robot software needs a framework}
% ===============================================================

\begin{frame}[fragile]{The obvious way to write it}
  \begin{pycodes}
while True:
    scan  = read_lidar()          # blocks 100 ms
    odom  = read_encoders()
    pose  = estimate(odom, scan)
    path  = plan(pose, goal)      # 200 ms, and not needed every cycle
    v, w  = control(path, pose)
    send_to_motors(v, w)
  \end{pycodes}

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \small This is correct, readable, and it is what everyone writes first.
  It is also unusable, for five separate reasons.
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
% One computed number, before the list of five. A list of five structural
% objections is easy to nod along to and impossible to remember; 7 cm of blind
% travel is neither.
\begin{frame}{Put a stopwatch on it}
  \small
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{5.2cm}r@{}}
    \toprule
    \texttt{read\_lidar()} --- one full sweep & 100\,ms \\
    \texttt{plan()} --- searching the map     & 200\,ms \\
    everything else                            & \phantom{0}20\,ms \\
    \midrule
    \textbf{one trip round the loop}           & \textbf{320\,ms} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.7em}
  So the controller runs at \textbf{3\,Hz}. You designed it for 20\,Hz.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \keyline{At 0.22\,m/s that is 7\,cm of travel between decisions --- half the robot's length,
  with its eyes shut.}

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \centering\footnotesize
  And planning did not need to run this cycle at all. Nor the one before it.
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Five reasons that loop fails}
  \small
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{One rate for everything.} The whole loop runs at the speed of its slowest
          line --- the 320\,ms you just measured. Your 20\,Hz controller runs at 3\,Hz.
    \item \textbf{One crash kills everything.} A malformed camera frame takes down the motor
          commands, and the robot keeps rolling with the last velocity it was given.
    \item \textbf{One machine.} You cannot move the heavy vision work to a second computer
          without rewriting the program.
    \item \textbf{Nothing is reusable.} Change the LiDAR model and you edit the same file
          that holds your controller.
    \item \textbf{You cannot see inside it.} When the robot turns the wrong way, there is
          nothing to inspect --- no way to ask ``what did the planner actually output?''
  \end{enumerate}

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \centering \small Every one of these is a \emph{structural} problem. No amount of careful
  coding inside that loop fixes them.
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{The answer: many small programs and a bus}
  \begin{center}

  \vspace{0.3em}
  \small
  Each box is a \textbf{separate operating-system process} with its own rate and its own
  crash. They exchange named, typed messages. Nothing calls anything directly ---
  \texttt{lidar\_driver} does not know the planner exists.

  \vspace{0.3em}
  This is a \textbf{robotics middleware}. The one we use is ROS\,2.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{ROS 2, honestly}
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \begin{block}{\small What it is}
      \footnotesize
      \begin{itemize}
        \item A way for many programs to exchange typed messages
        \item A build system and package format
        \item Command-line tools to inspect a running robot
        \item A large ecosystem of solved problems
      \end{itemize}
    \end{block}

    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \begin{block}{\small What it is \emph{not}}
      \footnotesize
      \begin{itemize}
        \item An operating system. The name is historical.
        \item Intelligence. It moves your data; it does not decide anything.
        \item A guarantee of anything. A badly designed graph is still badly designed.
      \end{itemize}
    \end{block}
  \end{columns}

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \begin{underhood}
    \footnotesize
    ROS\,2 is a rewrite, not an update. ROS\,1 had a single central master process --- if it
    died, the robot went deaf. ROS\,2 has no master: nodes discover each other directly.
    That change is why it is used in products and ROS\,1 largely was not.
  \end{underhood}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{A first look at a node}
  \small A ROS\,2 node that drives the robot forward. You will write this in Week 4 --- read
  it now for the \emph{shape}, not the detail.

  \begin{pycodes}
class DriveForward(Node):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__('drive_forward')
        self.pub = self.create_publisher(Twist, '/cmd_vel', 10)
        self.create_timer(0.1, self.tick)      # 10 Hz, forever

    def tick(self):
        msg = Twist()
        msg.linear.x = 0.2                     # 0.2 m/s forward
        self.pub.publish(msg)
  \end{pycodes}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \footnotesize
  No \texttt{while} loop. You declare \emph{what happens on each tick}, and the framework
  calls you. Every node in this course has this shape.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What we established today}
  \begin{enumerate}\small
    \item A robot \textbf{senses}, \textbf{decides} and \textbf{acts} --- all three, in a
          loop that never stops.
    \item Autonomy is not automation. Coping with an environment you did not control is the
          entire difficulty.
    \item Five blocks: perception, estimation, localization, planning, control. The rest of
          this course is one block at a time.
    \item Three hard truths: measurements are wrong, nothing is instantaneous, everything
          runs at once.
    \item Therefore robot software is \textbf{many small programs exchanging messages}, not
          one loop --- and that is what ROS\,2 gives us.
  \end{enumerate}
