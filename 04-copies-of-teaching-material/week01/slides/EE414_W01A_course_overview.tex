      You derive the kinematics.\\

\section{Why robotics is hard, and what we do about it}
% ===============================================================

\begin{frame}{Three reasons robotics is harder than the software you have written}
  \small
  \begin{table}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}p{3.1cm}p{4.0cm}p{4.4cm}@{}}
      \toprule
      \textbf{The difficulty} & \textbf{Why it bites} & \textbf{What this course does} \\
      \midrule
      The world is \newline uncertain
        & Your sensor reports 2.31\,m. The wall is at 2.28\,m. Neither number is wrong.
        & Estimation is taught as a first-class topic (Weeks 9--10), not an appendix. \\[0.4em]
      Everything runs \newline at once
        & Sensing, planning and control do not take turns. They all run, always, at
          different rates.
        & ROS\,2 from Week 1, so concurrency is the normal case and not a shock. \\[0.4em]
      There is no undo
        & A wrong loop prints wrong output. A wrong velocity command hits a wall.
        & Simulation first, every week. Hardware only once the code has earned it. \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{table}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{The gap this course exists to close}
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \column{0.47\textwidth}
    \textbf{What you can already do}
    \begin{itemize}\footnotesize
      \item Write a Python program that computes an answer
      \item Design a feedback controller on paper (EE 306)
      \item Analyse a system in the frequency domain
    \end{itemize}

    \column{0.47\textwidth}
    \textbf{What a robot needs}
    \begin{itemize}\footnotesize
      \item Ten programs running at once, talking to each other
      \item A controller whose input arrives late, noisy, and sometimes not at all
      \item A decision every 50\,ms, forever, with no human watching
    \end{itemize}
  \end{columns}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \begin{underhood}[The point]
    \footnotesize
    EE 306 gave you the controller. This course gives you everything between the controller
    and the wheels --- and that ``everything'' is where robots actually fail.
  \end{underhood}
\end{frame}

\section{Policies}
% ===============================================================

\begin{frame}{Attendance, integrity, and the rules that matter}
  \small
  \textbf{Attendance.} University policy, strictly applied. Attendance is taken in the first
  minutes. Missing a Lecture B session is expensive in a way that missing a lecture is not ---
  the environment moves forward and you do not.

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \textbf{Academic integrity.} Submitting another person's work as your own --- copied code,
  a repository forked without attribution, a paraphrased report --- brings course failure and
  possible suspension, per the university policy.

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \textbf{Open-source code.} Robotics runs on other people's packages, and using them is
  correct engineering. \textbf{Attribute it.} Name the package, the version and the licence
  in your report. Using \texttt{slam\_toolbox} is expected; presenting it as yours is
  plagiarism. We spend Week 13 on exactly this line.
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Using AI coding tools}
  \begin{beamercolorbox}[sep=8pt,center,rounded=true]{palette secondary}
    \small Permitted in assignments and the project. \textbf{Not} permitted in examinations.
  \end{beamercolorbox}

  \vspace{0.8em}
  Two conditions, and they are not negotiable:

  \begin{enumerate}\small
    \item \textbf{Disclose.} Say what you used and for what, in the report.
    \item \textbf{Defend.} Be able to explain and justify every line you submit, on the spot.
  \end{enumerate}

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \begin{inthefield}
    \footnotesize
    An assistant will happily give you ROS\,1 code, or code for a distribution three versions
    old, and it will look right. Debugging that is a skill this course teaches --- and the
    reason condition 2 exists. The exam is on paper for the same reason.
  \end{inthefield}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[fragile]{One setting that will save the whole room}
  \small
  ROS\,2 nodes find each other automatically over the network. In a shared lab, that means
  \textbf{your robot will be driven by the person sitting behind you} --- and neither of you
  will understand why.

  \begin{shells}
$ echo "export ROS_DOMAIN_ID=17" >> ~/.bashrc
$ source ~/.bashrc
  \end{shells}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  You will be assigned a number this week. Put it in your \texttt{.bashrc} today.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{underhood}
    \footnotesize
    The domain ID partitions the discovery traffic: nodes only see nodes on the same ID.
    It is the first thing to check when a node ``cannot see'' a topic that is obviously
    being published.
  \end{underhood}
\end{frame}
