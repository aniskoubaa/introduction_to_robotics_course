$ ros2 run ee414_course quaternion_demo

--------------------------------------------------------------------
1. A flat robot's quaternion IS its heading, written differently
--------------------------------------------------------------------
   A robot on a floor turns about one axis: straight up, z.
   So x and y are always 0, and only z and w carry the heading.

   heading        quaternion (x, y, z, w)                    back again
      0 deg      (0.000, 0.000, +0.0000, +1.0000)           +0.0 deg
     45 deg      (0.000, 0.000, +0.3827, +0.9239)          +45.0 deg
     90 deg      (0.000, 0.000, +0.7071, +0.7071)          +90.0 deg
    180 deg      (0.000, 0.000, +1.0000, +0.0000)         +180.0 deg
    -90 deg      (0.000, 0.000, -0.7071, +0.7071)          -90.0 deg

   Two of the four numbers are always zero and you still only ever
   had one angle. Nothing was lost and nothing was gained -- yet.
   The gain shows up in part 4, when the robot leaves the floor.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
2. Four numbers, one constraint
--------------------------------------------------------------------
   x^2 + y^2 + z^2 + w^2 = 1, always. That is what makes four
   numbers describe three degrees of freedom.

   a real heading of 37 deg -> (0.000, 0.000, 0.317305, 0.948324)
   its norm = 1.000000000000000

   now an invented one       -> (0.0, 0.0, 0.5, 0.5)
   its norm = 0.707106781186548   <-- not 1, so this is not a rotation
   it claims a heading of 45.0 deg, but the
   length is wrong, so it also scales anything it is applied to.

   divide by the norm        -> (0.000, 0.000, 0.707107, 0.707107)
   norm now = 1.000000000000000   <-- a rotation again, of 90.0 deg

--------------------------------------------------------------------
3. Drift, and how cheaply it is repaired
--------------------------------------------------------------------
   Odometry composes a small rotation thousands of times a minute.
   Every multiplication rounds. Watch the norm walk away from 1.

   composings        norm                      error from 1
           1        1.000000000000000       0.000e+00
         100        0.999999999999998       2.442e-15
        1000        0.999999999999976       2.409e-14
       10000        0.999999999999761       2.387e-13
      100000        0.999999999997585       2.415e-12
      200000        0.999999999995151       4.849e-12

   After 200000 compositions the norm is off by 4.849e-12.
   The repair is one line:

       q = q / norm(q)

   norm now = 1.000000000000000

   A 3x3 rotation matrix drifts too, but its constraint is that all
   three columns stay unit length AND mutually perpendicular -- six
   conditions, not one. Repairing it means re-orthonormalising, which
   is Gram-Schmidt or an SVD. That is the whole argument: tf2 stores
   quaternions because the cheap repair is the one you can afford to
   do every single cycle.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
4. Gimbal lock, watched as it happens
--------------------------------------------------------------------
   Roll-pitch-yaw recovers yaw with a division by cos(pitch).
   Tilt the pitch towards 90 degrees and watch that divisor go to zero.

   pitch        cos(pitch)          1/cos(pitch)
     0.000      1.000000e+00       1.000000e+00
    45.000      7.071068e-01       1.414214e+00
    80.000      1.736482e-01       5.758770e+00
    89.000      1.745241e-02       5.729869e+01
    89.900      1.745328e-03       5.729581e+02
    89.990      1.745329e-04       5.729578e+03
    89.999      1.745329e-05       5.729578e+04
    90.000      6.123e-17       DIVISION BY ZERO

   At pitch = 90 the first and third axes point the same way, so
   rolling and yawing do the identical thing. One degree of freedom
   has vanished from the representation -- not from the robot.

   The quaternion for that same orientation is ordinary:
       (x, y, z, w) = (0.0000, 0.7071, 0.0000, 0.7071)
       norm = 1.000000000000000

   No zero, no infinity, nothing special about it at all. There are
   no rings to line up, so there is nothing to lock.

   timestep      Euler finish            error        arc finish              error
    1.00 s     ( 1.2140,  0.7311)     3.437e-01    ( 1.0000,  1.0000)     1.110e-16
    0.50 s     ( 1.1135,  0.8703)     1.723e-01    ( 1.0000,  1.0000)     1.110e-16
    0.20 s     ( 1.0481,  0.9492)     6.996e-02    ( 1.0000,  1.0000)     1.110e-16
    0.10 s     ( 1.0245,  0.9748)     3.514e-02    ( 1.0000,  1.0000)     3.331e-16
    0.05 s     ( 1.0124,  0.9874)     1.765e-02    ( 1.0000,  1.0000)     2.220e-16
    0.02 s     ( 1.0050,  0.9950)     7.068e-03    ( 1.0000,  1.0000)     4.441e-16
    0.01 s     ( 1.0025,  0.9975)     3.534e-03    ( 1.0000,  1.0000)     3.331e-16

   Read the two error columns. Halving the timestep halves the Euler
   error -- it is first order, and it never reaches zero. The arc
   integrator is right to machine precision at every timestep,
   because holding v and omega constant IS an arc, and it follows one.

   This is why the error column matters: at 0.05 s, the rate a real
   robot actually runs its odometry, Euler is already off by
   centimetres on ONE quarter turn. Those centimetres accumulate
   for as long as the robot is switched on, and nothing removes them.
